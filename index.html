FPGA-Based Electrical Impedance Spectroscopy for Skin Tissue Characterization

Scope

Project Scope

We are building a system that sends a current through the skin and extracts the response at several frequencies. The result is an impedance spectrum and it gives information about the structure of the tissue underneath.

Why

Justification

Currently, suspicious lesions are commonly evaluated through excision biopsy, which provides tissue for histological diagnosis [1]. However, these procedures are invasive and can require extensive planning and post-procedure wound care to reduce risks such as bleeding and infection [2].

EIS provides a noninvasive method for quantitatively evaluating tissue electrical properties and could provide additional information when evaluating potentially abnormal lesions. This is particularly significant for melanoma because when detected early, the five-year survival rate is more than 99% [3]. Also, existing systems are not commercially available and are very expensive [4].

Our goal is to build an accessible, low-cost hardware that makes reliable quantitative skin measurements. Our prototype is not a diagnostic tool but rather a proof of concept and a foundation for future work on affordable tissue assessment.

System

Project Description

Our system uses the FPGA on a PYNQ-Z2 board to generate sine waves at 20 or more frequencies between 1 kHz and 100 kHz. A 12-bit DAC turns each wave into a small AC signal, and a front-end circuit limits the current before it reaches the skin. Four electrodes are placed on the skin: the outer two inject the current and the inner two measure the resulting voltage.

The response is digitized and sent back to the FPGA, which uses I/Q demodulation to find how large the signal is and how far it is shifted in time. The ARM processor on the same chip turns those numbers into a complex impedance at each frequency, corrects for circuit effects, and fits the Cole-Cole tissue model. The resulting spectrum and four fitted parameters appear on a dashboard in the browser after every sweep.

1. Generate the signal

The FPGA generates a digital wave and the samples go to a 12-bit DAC. During early testing, a bench function generator is used so we can verify the circuit first.

2. Apply it safely and measure the response

A current-limiting resistor and a DC-blocking capacitor control the signal before it reaches the skin. Two electrodes inject current while the other two measure voltage. A precision reference resistor in the loop tells us how much current is flowing.

3. Find magnitude and phase on the FPGA

The FPGA multiplies the digitized voltage and current by reference sine and cosine waves (I/Q demodulation). This pulls out how large each signal is and how far it is shifted in time.

4. Compute impedance and fit the model

The ARM processor computes the complex impedance at each frequency, corrects for circuit effects using a known resistor, and fits the Cole-Cole model. The model reduces the whole spectrum to four numbers: low- and high-frequency resistance, a time constant, and a spread factor.

5. Show the results

After each sweep, the board sends the spectrum and fitted parameters to a Flask dashboard in the browser.

Try the Cole-Cole model

Design

The whole system laid out: the hardware that touches the skin, the software that turns signals into numbers, and the limits that keep it safe.

Hardware

  • PYNQ-Z2 board with a Zynq FPGA and ARM processor
  • Pmod DA2 12-bit DAC and Pmod AD1 12-bit, 1 MSPS ADC
  • Two INA128 instrumentation amplifiers, gain of about 10
  • OPA2134 buffering a 1.65 V midpoint so signals stay in the ADC range
  • BAT85 diodes clamping ADC inputs between 0 and 3.3 V
  • Two 9 V batteries for an isolated ±9 V supply

Software

FPGA logic

A DDS sine generator sets each sweep frequency and streams samples to the Pmod DA2 over SPI. On the way back, it reads the Pmod AD1 and runs I/Q demodulation, multiplying the signals by reference sine and cosine waves to get magnitude and phase.

ARM processing

Python on the PYNQ-Z2’s ARM processor computes the complex impedance at each frequency, corrects for circuit effects using a known resistor, and fits the Cole-Cole model to the full spectrum.

Dashboard

After each sweep, the board sends the spectrum and fitted parameters to a Flask dashboard in the browser, so results can be seen right away.

Safety

Our current limits follow published skin-impedance work and sit at or below the IEC 60601-1 patient auxiliary current limits. Every frequency is checked on a 100 Ω test load with an oscilloscope before any skin contact, and we test only on ourselves as healthy volunteers. The device is battery powered and is not a certified medical device.
